# End users -- RFC 8890

**Text:** `rfc8890.txt`

## Scope

How the IETF weighs end users against other parties when their interests diverge, and what it owes
them when a design harms them.

**"End user" is narrow.** §2 means a human whose activities the standard supports -- and it makes the
point with a worked case: the end user of a router-management protocol is not the administrator, it
is the people on the network the router serves. Registrants, origin operators, CDNs, implementers,
Designated Experts and working groups are stakeholders, not end users. Most candidate findings die
here.

**This document takes a side.** §1 and §3 are explicit: the IETF is not value-neutral, and where
the interests of an end user conflict with another party's, the IAB argues the IETF should favour
the end user. §4.3 asks for harm to be examined, mitigated, and resolved in the user's favour -- not
merely recorded. If a design makes end users worse off so another party gains, say so. Softening
that into "the trade-off is undocumented" understates what the RFC asks.

**Not covered:** what the design should be instead. Name the trade and who bears it; do not
substitute your own preference for the WG's.

**Deferred, on the RFC's own instruction.** §4.3 names where this guidance has already been made
concrete. Three of the five it lists are lenses in this set: RFC 7754 (filtering), RFC 7258
(pervasive monitoring), RFC 6973 (privacy). Where one of them reaches the point, it will reach it
more concretely -- send it there.

## Firing

**Rarely -- because §2 is narrow, not because the bar is high.** Most drafts have no end user in
§2's sense at all, and that is what rules them out. Where there is one, take the finding seriously.

**First: are there end users in §2's sense?** Apply the router-administrator test. If every party in
the conflict is a stakeholder, stop.

**Then any one of these fires:**

- the design leaves end users worse off so that another party gains;
- the specification arbitrates between end users and another party and picks a side without saying
  so;
- the document claims to serve end users where the mechanism does not.

A charter, architecture statement or policy document is not a fourth trigger. It is a reason to look
harder at the three, since a document that sets policy is where a trade gets made silently. As a
trigger it fires on every BCP, and only the §2 test above stops it.

### Where this ends and RFC 3552 begins

RFC 3552 asks that residual risk be *described*. This asks that the trade go the *right way*. They
are different findings and the distinction decides which lens carries it:

- "the document never states who bears this" -- RFC 3552.
- "the document states it, and end users bear it so another party need not" -- here.

Rule this lens out where the finding is only that something is unwritten and RFC 3552 or the actor
table already carries it more concretely. Do not rule it out because the point sounds like an
opinion; §1's whole claim is that this one is the IETF's.

### Versus only looking like it

The recurring failure is a claim about the *topic* rather than the *document*. "This design gives
providers too much power" is a position. "§4.2 lets the provider choose which key is served, the
user cannot detect the choice, and §7 treats only the provider's risk" is a finding -- and its
conclusion may legitimately be that the design is wrong for users.

§4.3 sets the evidentiary bar in both directions: asserting harm is not adequate, and neither is
declining to look. Ground it in the mechanism, name who loses, then say so plainly.

It fires misleadingly where the served party and the affected party are the same population, which
is the common case.

The lens can also come back *supporting* the design. §4.5 asks that editor convenience, process
formality and architectural purity not be prioritised over end users -- a document that chooses
deployed reality over process neatness is going the direction §4.5 wants, and saying so is a result.

## What this lens asks

- Are there end users here at all, in §2's sense?
- Does the document resolve a conflict between end users and another party -- and does the resolution
  go their way?
- Who gains from the trade, and who bears it?
- Does it claim to serve a party the mechanism gives nothing to?
- Does the architecture create a role that represents the end user's interests, or leave them
  represented only by a party with different ones?

## Where to look

- **§2** -- who counts as an end user, with the router-administrator example. The section that rules
  most candidates out.
- **§4.3** -- the operative clause. End-user-versus-stakeholder conflict, the instruction to err
  towards end users, the bar on merely asserting harm, the pointer to RFC 7754 / RFC 7258 / RFC 6973,
  and concentration of power.
- **§4.2** -- architectures that do or do not create a role representing end users; the user-agent
  role as the worked example, and IoT as the counter-example. The section to reach for when the
  finding is about a missing role rather than a stated trade-off.
- **§4.4** -- conflicting needs *between end users*, and the duty to document the trade-off where one
  is unavoidable. Narrow: it does not reach stakeholder conflicts.
- **§4.5** -- internal needs that must not outrank end users: document-editor convenience, IETF
  process, architectural purity. Often runs in the design's favour.
