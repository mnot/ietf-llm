# Pervasive monitoring — RFC 7258 (BCP 188)

**Text:** `rfc7258.txt`

Short — 333 lines. Read the whole thing; locating a section costs about as much.

## Scope

Surveillance that is indiscriminate and very large scale, and the IETF's commitment to make it more
expensive or infeasible by design.

A **BCP**, recording IETF consensus — unlike most lenses in this set, it has standing. But it asks
for consideration, not a section: §2 says no "pervasive monitoring considerations" section is
needed.

**"Attack" is technical here.** §1 defines it as subverting the intent of the communicating parties
without their agreement, and says the term implies nothing about the motivation of the actor — which
may be a nation state, a commercial enterprise acting legally, or a criminal. So a finding does not
have to establish that anyone intends harm, and "nobody would do that" is not an answer.

**Not covered:** targeted interception, which is RFC 2804; per-party data exposure, which is
RFC 6973.

## Firing

The design creates or widens a vantage point from which many users' activity can be observed —
*indiscriminately and at very large scale*. The scale qualifier is the whole test and it is what
most often rules candidates out.

The artefacts count as much as the content: §1 includes protocol metadata such as headers, traffic
analysis (correlation, timing, packet sizes), and subversion of the keys protecting a protocol.
A design that leaks none of the payload can still fire.

### Versus only looking like it

Almost any protocol creates *some* observation point, so the pull toward firing is constant and
usually wrong. Two things distinguish a real hit: the observer is not a party the user chose to
contact, and the exposure is bounded only by the size of the deployment. A first-party disclosure to
a server you deliberately connected to is not pervasive monitoring, however sensitive. Neither is an
exposure confined to one administrative domain.

**Monitoring is not automatically the attack.** §2 names network management, anti-spam, and
certificate transparency as monitoring that is beneficial or is itself a mitigation, and says
plainly that making networks unmanageable is not an acceptable outcome — while ignoring PM goes
against the consensus. Where a design's observability is the operator's own management data, the
finding is about the tension, not about the observation.

Where the lens fires weakly, RFC 6973 usually carries the finding better, and citing both is
padding. Say so in the result rather than citing thinly.

## What this lens asks

- Does the design let one party observe many users, or one user more thoroughly?
- Is the observation a by-product of the architecture, or does someone have to choose to collect?
- What does it expose in metadata, in traffic shape, and in key material — not just in content?
- Does an architectural choice, particularly the reuse of existing technology, carry a monitoring
  exposure the document does not mention?
- If the draft raises pervasive monitoring as an argument *against* an alternative, has it turned
  the same analysis on itself?

## Where to look

- **§1** — the definition and the scale qualifier that bounds it, the enumerated techniques, and the
  technical sense of "attack". Read the qualifier before deciding this lens fires.
- **§2** — everything else, and it is one page:
  - the obligation. Not a section; an answer, if asked, to whether PM is relevant here and how it
    was considered.
  - what existing security standards do *not* address — confidentiality of protocol metadata,
    countering traffic analysis, data minimisation. A serviceable checklist.
  - architectural decisions, including which existing technology gets reused, as the place PM
    exposure is decided; early review matters because revisiting late is very costly. This is the
    argument for raising it during review rather than after.
  - what mitigation means: not prevention, but raising the cost, forcing covert to overt, or making
    the attack more likely to be detected. The vocabulary for what to ask the authors for.
  - beneficial monitoring, and the balance to strike.
  - the scope limits — the IETF does not control implementation or deployment, does not standardise
    every layer, and the legal and political aspects are out of scope. Useful for what not to raise.
