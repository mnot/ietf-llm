# Filtering and blocking -- RFC 7754

**Text:** `rfc7754.txt`

## Scope

Deliberate blocking of a communication by a party other than the endpoints: who decides, where it is
enforced, and what the enforcement costs everyone else.

**Not covered:** interception, which is RFC 2804. Filtering the user chose and controls is in scope
but rarely a finding.

**Not an objection to filtering.** §3.2 lists legitimate motivations -- security response, content
restriction under law or policy, parental controls. The document's position is that filtering will
continue and the question is where and how, not whether. A finding that reads as opposition to
blocking as such has missed the document.

An IAB-stream informational document. It carries no requirements; cite it for the analysis, not for
a violation.

## Firing

Something in the design decides whether a communication proceeds -- or the design creates a point at
which a third party could make that decision for others.

### Versus only looking like it

This is about a party *other than the endpoints* deciding. It does not fire on a protocol's own
internal arbitration: a node ignoring a misbehaving peer, an algorithm-negotiation failure, a rate
limit, a cache declining to serve. Those are ordinary protocol behaviour, and reaching for RFC 7754
there overstates.

Nor does it fire where the endpoint is the enforcement point and chose to be. §6 says that is the
*preferred* arrangement, so a design that puts the decision at a cooperative endpoint is on the
right side and the lens should say so.

In practice the actionable content often turns out to be the same as some other finding -- most
commonly "the design provides no way to distinguish failure from suppression", which belongs with
the response-semantics issue. Folding it there and recording that the lens was opened is the honest
outcome, and it happens often.

## What this lens asks

- Who decides, and does the affected party know a decision was made?
- Is the enforcement point the endpoint, or somewhere in the network that cannot see the context?
- If the mechanism is suppressed, can a participant tell suppression from absence?
- Does a transparency or appeal path exist, and does it reach the party affected?
- Does the mechanism require an intermediary in the secure path, and what does that cost?

## Where to look

**The framework** -- §4.1's four criteria are the tool. Apply them rather than arguing in general
terms.

- **§4.1.1 Scope** -- which hosts and users are affected, including those nobody meant to affect.
- **§4.1.2 Granularity** -- whether blocking one thing takes others with it.
- **§4.1.3 Efficacy** -- how easily the target evades it.
- **§4.1.4 Security** -- the strongest of the four for a protocol review. Blocking that requires an
  intermediary in the end-to-end path breaks the property TLS and IPsec exist to provide, and leaves
  endpoints unable to distinguish an authorised blocker from an attacker. It also carries the
  counterproductive case: a mechanism whose behaviour lets users *locate* what was blocked.

**The patterns** -- §4.2, §4.3 and §4.4 apply those four criteria to network-based,
rendezvous-based and endpoint-based blocking. Read the one that matches the design.

- **§4.3** -- rendezvous-based, where the design has a lookup or discovery step someone could
  suppress. §4.3.4 for what it does to security.
- **§4.4** -- endpoint-based, and §4.4.5 for the server-endpoint case.
- **§4.2** -- network-based, the pattern that scores worst on the criteria.

**The position**

- **§6** -- the conclusion: filter at the endpoint where possible, because cooperative endpoints have
  the context to target it; and where filtering happens elsewhere, tell the endpoint promptly, so
  errors and collateral damage can be redressed. This is the yardstick for network-side enforcement
  and the source of the transparency ask.
- **§3.1–§3.4** -- who sets policy, for what purposes, against whom, using what. §3.2 is what stops
  the lens becoming an objection to filtering as such.
