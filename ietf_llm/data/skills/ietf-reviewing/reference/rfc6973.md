# Privacy considerations -- RFC 6973

**Text:** `rfc6973.txt`

## Scope

What a protocol exposes about identifiable people, to whom, and what the design could do about it.

IAB-stream informational. §7 says outright that it recommends no specific practices and is not
directions for writing a privacy considerations section -- it is a questionnaire to think with. So a
finding cites an unanswered question, not a violated requirement.

**Not covered:** mass observation at internet scale, which is RFC 7258 and a narrower, harder test;
whether a party learns more than its function needs, framed as a partitioning remedy, which is
RFC 9614. Security is deliberately thin here -- §7 defers it to RFC 3552.

**How far a finding may reach.** §2 concedes that designers cannot foresee every privacy
implication, and asks them to consider interaction with systems outside the protocol *without*
imagining every possible deployment. A finding built on an exotic hypothetical deployment is outside
what the document asks for.

## Firing

The draft handles data about identifiable people, or emits per-user events that someone other than
the user can observe. "Identifiable" is broader than "identified": a stable pseudonym, a device
identifier, or an address counts.

### Versus only looking like it

A draft that exposes something and says so has engaged this lens. A draft that asserts
"privacy-preserving" or "metadata-only" without argument has not, and that gap is the finding.

Where the draft's own introduction makes a privacy argument against an alternative, the symmetric
analysis of its own design is owed.

Answering with "the deployment can configure this" is not an answer to §7.1(c) -- that question asks
specifically whether implementers *and* operators have such controls, so name them or record their
absence.

## What this lens asks

- What does the protocol expose about individuals, their devices, or their use of them?
- To *which* entity is each thing exposed? Use §3.1's vocabulary -- initiator, recipient,
  intermediary, enabler, observer, attacker -- not "observers" as a mass noun.
- Can the subject learn what is held about them, or contest it?
- Does the draft claim a privacy property it has not argued for?
- Where the design trades privacy for something else, is the trade stated?
- Does the *default* configuration minimise exposure, and if not, is the reason given?

## Where to look

**§7 is the instrument, and it is lettered.** Cite the letter -- `§7.1(c)`, not `§7.1` -- so a reader
can check the claim. Its four subsections mirror §6's mitigation classes plus a general one.

- **§7.1 Data Minimization**, a–g: (a) identifiers and whether they allow correlation; (b) what is
  exposed about individuals and their devices; **(c) Observers -- which of (a) and (b) reaches each
  protocol entity, and whether implementers or operators can limit it**; (d) fingerprinting;
  (e) persistence, deletion and expiry of identifiers; (f) correlation with data from outside the
  protocol; (g) retention. (c) is the question most reviews end up on.
- **§7.2 User Participation**, a–d: consent mechanisms, and whether the initiator can share
  different things with different recipients, limit what intermediaries see, or express a preference.
- **§7.3 Security** -- surveillance and traffic analysis, stored-data compromise. Thin by design;
  RFC 3552 carries it.
- **§7.4 General**: (a) trade-offs, and the rationale for the one chosen; **(b) defaults -- whether
  the default mode minimises amount, identifiability and persistence, maximises user participation,
  and takes the strictest security options, with an explanation if not.** (b) is decidable against
  most drafts and is rarely answered.

**The vocabulary**

- **§3.1** -- the entity definitions that make "to which entity" answerable: initiator, recipient,
  intermediary (in the path and necessary), enabler (facilitates without being in the path),
  observer, attacker, eavesdropper, individual. §4 elaborates them.

**The threats**, when a concern needs naming rather than a question answering

- **§5.2.1 Correlation** and **§5.2.2 Identification** -- linkage across contexts or over time.
- **§5.2.3 Secondary Use** and **§5.2.4 Disclosure** -- data used or revealed beyond its purpose.
- **§5.2.5 Exclusion** -- the subject cannot see or contest what is held about them.
- **§5.1.1 Surveillance** -- a third party observes rather than participates.

**The remedies**

- **§6.1 Data Minimization**, with §6.1.1–§6.1.4 on anonymity, pseudonymity, identity
  confidentiality. Where the finding needs an ask rather than an objection, it is here.
- **§6.2 User Participation** -- the mitigation §7.2 questions.
