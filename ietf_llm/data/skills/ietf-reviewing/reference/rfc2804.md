# Wiretapping -- RFC 2804

**Text:** `rfc2804.txt`

Short -- 557 lines, and §3 is the only part most reviews need.

## Scope

The IETF's decision not to consider wiretapping requirements when making standards, and a technical
definition of what wiretapping is.

Joint IAB/IESG, informational. It is a *policy* statement about what the IETF will and will not
design, not a set of protocol requirements -- so a finding is about the document's fit with that
policy, not a violation.

**Not covered:** observation by a party the user contacted, which is RFC 6973; monitoring at scale,
which is RFC 7258. §3's own worked example draws the second line -- random monitoring of a large
population fails condition 4, so it is not wiretapping by this definition even though RFC 7258
treats it as an attack.

## Firing

The design lets a party other than the endpoints obtain content or metadata, or makes an endpoint
report on its own user. Check §3 before concluding it fires.

### The definition, in four conditions

Information passed from one party to one or more others is delivered to a **third** party:

1. without the sending party knowing about the third party;
2. without any recipient knowing about the delivery;
3. when the sender's normal expectation is that only the recipients, or parties obliged to keep it
   confidential, will see it;
4. when the third party acts **deliberately to target** that transmission, because the sender or the
   recipient is of interest.

"Party" covers a person, a group, or equipment acting for them.

§3's own worked failures are the fastest way to kill a candidate: monitoring public newsgroups
fails 3; random monitoring of a large population fails 4; a recipient forwarding private mail
fails 2.

Two extensions worth knowing. The definition covers the whole of capture → filtering → delivery, so
broad collection later narrowed to a small group *is* wiretapping. And accounting logs used for call
tracing count, because the sender expects the accounting party to hold them in confidence.

The definition deliberately excludes three things, so do not argue about them: whether the tap is
lawful, whether it is real-time or after the fact, and what medium is targeted.

### Versus only looking like it

**This lens is built and then killed more often than any other, and §3 is why.** A provider that
could substitute a key, or a server that sees a request it was deliberately sent, generally fails
the test -- the party is not third, or the sender knows.

Two things usually survive when the definition does not:

- **§1's venue argument**, where a design takes its content from a jurisdiction. That is an
  institutional finding, and often a strong one.
- **§4's dual-use observation**, as support for a concern about scope or applicability -- not as an
  accusation about intent.

Recording that you opened §3 and it did not fit is worth a line; the next reviewer will otherwise
build the same argument again.

## What this lens asks

- Does a third party obtain the communication, and does the sender know?
- Is the capability designed in, or merely a consequence of someone being trusted anyway?
- If the draft depends on national law or a jurisdictional authority, is that dependency stated?
- Where the capability exists but is not the purpose, is it at least openly described?

## Where to look

- **§3** -- the definition and its four conditions, the capture/filter/deliver framing, the worked
  failures, and what the definition deliberately ignores. Read it first; most candidates die here.
- **§1** -- the position, in six bullets. Two carry findings: the IETF is the wrong forum for
  features arising from the laws of individual countries, since bodies whose authority matches one
  jurisdiction are more appropriate; and mechanisms that facilitate or enable wiretapping *should be
  openly described*, because publication maximises review. The others argue that added wiretap
  requirements increase complexity and so jeopardise security, and restate RFC 1984 on strong
  cryptography.
- **§4** -- why no moral position is taken, and that tools built for one purpose get used for others.
- **§5** -- utility. Copying bytes between static endpoints is easy; correlating identity with point
  of attachment is much harder; simple countermeasures defeat it; encrypted content leaves only
  traffic analysis. Useful where a draft claims a capability is necessary or effective.
